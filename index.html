One consequence to carry into everything below. Q is a ratio whose denominator is the task's goal-predicate count, and that count is often 1. Partial credit is available on paper across the benchmark, and absent in practice on a quarter of it.

Diffusion Policy is the worst-fitted and the cleanest control. Five of eight rows fall outside what it has been shown to handle, and the one thing that matches exactly — 200 demonstrations per task — is the least interesting. But it is the only one of the three that does not need language, so the missing instructions cost it nothing, and it is small enough to train per-task on one workstation GPU. That makes it a poor submission candidate and a useful control. The paper's action-horizon result was measured on {DDPM, 300–700-step episodes}; a π0.5 result here would be {flow matching, 10,000-step episodes}, so architecture and task regime move together and cannot be told apart. Training a Diffusion Policy on a BEHAVIOR task fills the missing cell: it differs from the paper in regime only, and from the π0.5 arm in architecture only. If both peak at the same horizon, the architecture is not what changed the answer — two maximally different models agreeing is hard to explain any other way. What that does not license is the conclusion that the task regime is responsible. BEHAVIOR differs from Push-T in at least four ways at once, and both policies inherit all of them: episode length, a mixed action space with arms on absolute position and the base on velocity, assisted grasping, and 30 Hz control. The velocity-integration effect is a property of the robot, not the architecture, so it would hit both models identically. Agreement rules out the architecture; isolating the regime needs a further arm that holds the robot fixed and varies only episode length.
Comet pt50 is the best-fitted and the most constrained. It was trained on behavior-1k/2025-challenge-demos, and the 50 tasks it saw are carried into 2026 with their demonstration lengths unchanged — not one carried task moved. So half the 2026 benchmark is in-distribution for it. The other half is not, and neither are the four scenes that host only new tasks.
The resolution finding cuts against intuition. The evaluator renders 979,200 RGB pixels per step and the serving wrapper immediately resizes them to 224². The measured benefit of full-resolution rendering therefore cannot be extra detail reaching the policy — the model sees the same 224² either way. The plausible mechanism is downsampling quality: a 720² render reduced to 224² is better antialiased than a native 224² render. That is testable and untested.
What looks wrong

Six things that do not behave as documented

Ordered by how much each one should change a decision. Every claim below cites the file it came from; nothing here rests on a docstring or a README.

  1. Task durations do not match the demos, for the new 50 only
    All 50 carried-over tasks agree with the evaluator's demo length to within a second. Of the 50 new ones, two do. Median error 13.3 %, worst +31.6 % on cook_brussels_sprouts. Scoring is unaffected — evaluator.py:163 reads length from task.jsonl, never this field — but anything that plans from duration is wrong on exactly the half of the benchmark nobody has run. Task by task below.
    source task_data.json vs datasets/2026-challenge-task-instances/metadata/task.jsonl · evaluator.py:163
  2. 24 tasks have exactly one goal predicate, so Q is binary
    Q-score is len(satisfied)/(len(satisfied)+len(unsatisfied)). On a one-predicate task there is no partial credit at all — Q is 0 or 1. That includes turning_on_radio, the task every published baseline is tuned on. Across the benchmark the median task has just 3 goal predicates.
    source behavior_task.py:410 · bddl3/bddl/activity_definitions/*/problem0.bddl
  3. More goal predicates means a lower score, not more partial credit
    If partial credit worked as designed, tasks with more predicates would yield more Q to a partly-capable policy. The 2025 field shows the opposite: Spearman -0.215 between predicate count and achieved Q. The predicates are effectively conjunctive and sequential — you cannot satisfy the fifth without the first four — so the metric behaves closer to all-or-nothing than its design implies. Treat the size with suspicion. The predictor is a syntactic count, and it undercounts the 66 tasks whose goals are quantified (see Method). Correcting those counts would move tasks rightward by an unknown amount. The direction is unlikely to flip, since the hardest tasks are quantified too, but −0.215 is not a number to quote precisely.
    source 23 submissions in docs/challenge_submissions/ · per_task_scores.q_score
  4. The new 50 tasks have no high-level instruction, though the dataset is richly annotated
    All 50 carried-over tasks carry an instruction string in task_data.json; all 50 new ones have none. meta/tasks.parquet holds only a task index, and the per-episode tasks field is the bare snake_case name — 100 distinct values across all 20,000 episodes, not 20,000 annotations. But the dataset does supply language, in a place none of those three look. annotations/ is the largest directory in the release — 20,002 files, one JSON per episode for all 100 tasks — and each one carries per-segment skill text with frame ranges. For vacuuming_floors, one of the new 50: "move to" [0,416] on ["vacuum"], then "pick up from" [416,592] on ["vacuum","floors"], each tagged with a skill type. So the gap is specifically the one-sentence task instruction, not language as such. An earlier version of this page concluded there was no language for the new 50 anywhere; that was wrong, and it was wrong because the check covered only meta/.
    source task_data.json · meta/tasks.parquet · meta/episodes/chunk-*/file-*.parquet · annotations/task-0069/episode_00690030.json · HF list_repo_files
  5. One training demo is 128× shorter than its task's median
    Episode 544 of putting_away_Halloween_decorations runs 148 steps — 4.9 seconds — against a median of 13,924 steps (7.7 minutes) for that task. Ten episodes across the corpus fall under 40 seconds. It is not a bookkeeping error: all six video streams for that episode — three RGB, three depth — span 4.93 s, matching length/30 exactly, while a median episode of the same task spans 464.03 s and matches equally well. Episodes share packed video files addressed by timestamp range, so this is not a missing file either. The task has its full 200 episodes. The recording really is 4.9 seconds, and it is in the training set.
    source meta/episodes/chunk-002/file-000.parquet · 20,000 episodes scanned
  6. The five tasks nobody scored on in 2025 share one predicate
    wash_dog_toys, clean_a_patio, clean_a_trumpet, cook_cabbage and make_pizza drew Q = 0.000 from every one of the 23 submissions. All 23 report all five tasks, so this is not an artefact of missing entries, and there is no nonzero value in either q_score or task_sr anywhere in the set. It holds in all four subgroups — including the privileged track, whose submissions may read ground-truth simulator state. Perception is therefore not the binding constraint on these five. Three of the five are scored on covered, which appears in 60 % of the zero-score tasks against 5 % of the solved ones. covered is a particle-system state — dust, water, stains. The field did not fail at long horizons here; it failed at fluids.

Progress

Which predicates pay out, and how far anyone gets

Each of the 23 submissions (listed under Provenance) reports a Q for every individual rollout. Because Q is satisfied ÷ total, a value on an N-predicate task decodes to exactly how many predicates that rollout achieved. 3,256 rollouts decode this way.

inside is where partial credit lives; ontop is where tasks get finished. Both start at about two thirds of rollouts earning something, but ontop completes at 37.7 % against inside's 14.2 %. Placing objects on things is a skill the field has; placing them in things it can begin and seldom complete.
covered and attached barely start. Under 19 % of rollouts earn any credit on either. For attached, any-credit and completion are the same number — it never partially succeeds. These are the particle and fastening states, and they are the benchmark's floor.
What this cannot tell you. Q gives the count of satisfied predicates, never which ones. On a mixed-type task, 1 of 4 does not say which of the four. True satisfaction order needs per-frame replay — a replay harness of this kind (extract_labels.py, written for this workspace) already does exactly this, recording satisfied and unsatisfied predicate indices per frame and timestamping each flip, but it has only ever been run on two tasks. Extending it to the benchmark is a GPU job, and it is the only way to answer which predicate comes first.

Manipulation, not duration. How far the hands must travel predicts difficulty better than how long the task takes, and much better than how many conditions it has. Discussion

One team scored better on the hidden set

Four of the five teams with both submissions dropped on the hidden set, as you would expect. Comet rose 37 %. Comet's checkpoints are publicly released, so the distinction is worth stating precisely for anyone using them.

The gain is broad, not a couple of lucky tasks. Across the 50 tasks, Comet improved on 27, worsened on 14 and matched on 9; the five largest gainers account for only 36 % of the total gain. So it is not an outlier effect.

But it gained partial credit while losing completions. Q rose 37 % and task success rate fell 21 %. The tasks that improved went from exactly 0.000 to partial — collecting_childrens_toys 0.000→0.583, storing_food 0.000→0.400 — while the tasks that regressed were ones it had fully solved: turning_on_radio and cook_hot_dogs both 1.000→0.700.

And it drove far further. Base distance, normalised by the human demonstration, went from 0.53 to 1.93 — a 265 % rise, against 1.12→1.28 for Robot Learning Collective on the same pair of sets. Time score fell 22 %. The picture is a policy covering much more ground, touching more objects, satisfying the first predicate of more tasks, and finishing fewer of them.

What would explain it is not determinable from the published files. If the hidden instances simply favour this method — trading completion for coverage — the other four teams with both submissions might be expected to show some version of the same shift, and none do. The overall-score files carry no information about configuration, so the cause is left open here rather than guessed at.
For anyone using the released checkpoints, the public figure is 0.1830. Comet is widely cited at Q 0.2514, which is the hidden-set result. Evaluated on the public instances — the ones the challenge distributes — their own submission scored 0.1830, so a reproduction measured there and compared against 0.2514 reads a 27 % regression that is not real. On the hidden set the top score was Robot Learning Collective's 0.2599, with Comet second at 0.2514.
Method

How the predicates were counted, and why the count is a floor

Goal predicates were counted by parsing the (:goal …) block of each problem0.bddl and taking the leaf predicates. For a goal like turning_on_radio's, that is exact:

(:goal (and (toggled_on ?radio_receiver.n.01_1)))

One predicate written, one predicate scored. But 66 of the 100 goal blocks contain a quantifier, and a quantifier is a template rather than a predicate. picking_up_trash reads:

(:goal (and
  (forall (?can__of__soda.n.01 - can__of__soda.n.01)
    (inside ?can__of__soda.n.01 ?ashcan.n.01_1))))

That is every can of soda is inside the ashcan. The parse counts one. The :objects block in the same file declares three cans, so at runtime goal_status holds three ground predicates and binning two of them scores 0.67 — real partial credit the written block does not reveal. This is ordinary BDDL semantics, not a defect, which is why it is here and not in the findings.

Two consequences for the numbers on this page. Every predicate count marked + is a floor. And the denominator can differ between instances of the same task, because each of the 40 test instances is a different scene layout — three cans on one, five on another, scored out of three and out of five.

The fix, if the numbers need to be exact. Load each task instance in the simulator and read goal_status directly. About 100 scene loads at 150–300 s each, so 4–8 hours on one GPU. That would replace every + with a real number and let the predicate correlation be recomputed on counts that are actually right.
